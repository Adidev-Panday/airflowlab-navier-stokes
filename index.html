Seeing the Navier-Stokes equations

Every wing, weather system, and river obeys one set of equations. This wind tunnel solves a version of them live, so you can watch orderly flow break down into turbulence — the exact phenomenon behind a million-dollar open problem in mathematics.

The equations

The Navier-Stokes equations say that a fluid's momentum changes because of three things: the fluid pushing itself around, pressure differences, and internal friction (viscosity).

Here u is the velocity of the air at every point, p is pressure, ρ is density, ν is viscosity, and f is any external force. The second equation just says the air isn't compressed — what flows in flows out.

The Reynolds number is the dial that matters

The single number that decides whether flow stays smooth or turns chaotic is the Reynolds number: how strongly the air's own motion dominates over its internal friction.

Low Re means friction wins and flow stays laminar and glassy. High Re means the fluid's own momentum wins, and you get the swirling, shedding vortices you can trigger with the slider. Turn the Reynolds dial up and watch the wake fall apart into a repeating trail of vortices — a von Kármán vortex street.

Why this is a Millennium Problem

The Clay Mathematics Institute offers a $1,000,000 prize for the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem. In three dimensions, nobody has been able to prove that smooth starting conditions always stay smooth forever — or find a case where the maths "blows up" into an infinite value. Turbulence, the thing you see forming in the wake here, is exactly what makes this so hard: the equations resist every known method.

In September 2026 this made global news when a proof was announced for a forced version of the 3D problem, and the Clay Institute said the problem had "apparently been settled." Credit and verification are still being worked through, and the classic unforced turbulence question that matters most for real aircraft is still not fully closed. So this remains one of the most alive problems in physics and maths.

What this simulation is (and isn't)

  • It solves a real 2D incompressible fluid model in your browser, in real time, using the "stable fluids" method with vorticity confinement to keep vortices crisp.
  • The Reynolds number is indicative: it maps to the solver's viscosity so you can see the laminar-to-turbulent transition, not to publish a precise drag figure.
  • It is a teaching visualiser — the goal is intuition about why turbulence is hard, not an engineering-grade CFD result.

Built for the Woodstock School physics department as a teaching tool. Airflow over a NACA-style airfoil; drag to orbit the tunnel, adjust the wing's angle, and push the Reynolds number to feel where smooth flow ends.
